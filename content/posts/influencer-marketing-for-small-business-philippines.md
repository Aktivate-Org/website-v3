Influencer marketing for small business in the Philippines doesn't start with a celebrity or a big agency retainer. For a milk tea shop in Iloilo, a skincare brand selling on Shopee or a resort in La Union, it usually starts with a handful of local creators whose followers already trust them, a clear offer, and a way to see what sold.

This guide walks through that, step by step: setting a goal, picking a platform, finding creators, writing a brief, paying fairly, tracking results and keeping your campaign compliant.

## Why it works so well in the Philippines

Filipinos spend a lot of their online life on social platforms, and they pay attention to creators.

- DataReportal's [Digital 2027: The Philippines](https://datareportal.com/reports/digital-2027-philippines) counted **94.7 million social media user identities in October 2026**, equal to 80.3% of the population.
- The same report puts the ad reach of **Facebook at 94.7 million**, **TikTok at 86.4 million adults (18+)**, **YouTube at 62.0 million** and **Instagram at 29.8 million**. These are advertising-reach estimates, not unique people, but they show where your customers are.
- Meltwater's [Digital 2026 Philippines highlights](https://learn.meltwater.com/apac-en-report-2026_phillipines_digital_report-2025-12_DownloadReport.html) found that **45% of users follow influencers**, about twice the global average of 22%.

For a small business, that's the opening: your customers already take recommendations from creators. You just need the right ones, talking to the right people.

## Step 1: Pick one goal and one offer

Before you message anyone, decide what a good result looks like. Pick one:

- **Sales:** orders through a link, a discount code or your online shop.
- **Foot traffic:** people visiting your café, salon or store in a specific city.
- **Awareness for a launch:** a new product, a new branch or a seasonal menu.

Then give creators something worth talking about: a launch, a bundle, a limited discount for their followers. Time it with the moments Filipinos already shop for, such as payday weekends, the 11.11 and 12.12 sales, and the long Christmas season.

## Step 2: Choose the platform your customers use

- **Facebook** has the widest reach and still works well for local businesses, groups and community pages.
- **TikTok** is where short demos, food reviews and "come with me" videos travel fastest.
- **Instagram** is smaller, but strong for fashion, beauty, cafés and lifestyle.
- **YouTube** suits longer reviews, tutorials and anything people research before buying.

Start with one or two platforms, not all of them.

## Step 3: Find local creators who fit

For most small businesses, **nano (roughly 1,000–10,000 followers) and micro creators (roughly 10,000–100,000)** are the sweet spot: their audiences are often local, they reply to comments, and their rates fit an SME budget.

Ways to find them:

- **Start with your own customers and followers.** People who already tag you are your warmest leads.
- **Search by place and niche:** "Cebu food", "Davao café", "QC skincare" on TikTok and Instagram.
- **See who posts about your competitors** and businesses near you.
- **Check the comments, not just the follower count.** Real conversations beat big numbers. Use our [engagement rate calculator](/tools/engagement-rate-calculator/) to compare creators quickly.

Shortlist five to ten creators. Look at their last 10–15 posts: do their followers look like your customers, and would your product sit naturally in their content?

## Step 4: Write a simple brief

A one-page brief saves you from disappointing posts. Include:

- **The goal and the offer** (e.g. "drive orders for our new bundle, 15% off with code LINA15").
- **Three things they must mention** and anything they must not say.
- **Format and timing:** for example, one TikTok and three Stories within a week.
- **Disclosure:** that the post must be clearly marked as paid (see the rules below).
- **Approval:** whether you want to see the post before it goes live.
- **Usage rights:** whether you can repost the video or use it in ads, and for how long.

Leave room for the creator's own style. Their followers trust their voice, not your script.

## Step 5: Agree on payment

Small businesses usually pay in one of three ways:

- **A fixed fee** per post or package.
- **Product or experience** (a free meal, a treatment, a stay), often for smaller creators.
- **Commission** on sales through their link or code, sometimes on top of a small fee.

Rates vary with platform, niche, engagement and how much work the content takes. For a starting point in pesos, use our [influencer rate calculator for brands in the Philippines](/tools/influencer-rate-calculator/?country=PH&mode=brand). Put what you agree in writing, even if it's a short message: deliverables, dates, payment, disclosure and usage rights.

## Step 6: Track what actually sold

Give each creator their own discount code or link so you can see who drove what. Track:

- **Orders or bookings** from each code or link.
- **Messages and inquiries** that mention the creator.
- **Saves, shares and comments,** which tell you whose content people found useful.

After the first round, keep working with the creators who performed. Long-term partners usually get better results than one-off posts, because their followers see your product more than once.

## Stay on the right side of the rules

Influencer posts are advertising, and the usual consumer rules apply. A [summary of Philippine laws on influencer marketing by Respicio & Co.](https://www.respicio.ph/commentaries/philippine-laws-on-social-media-influencer-marketing-endorsements-and-consumer-protection) highlights:

- **The Consumer Act (RA 7394)** prohibits deceptive, unfair and unconscionable sales practices, so claims in a creator's post must be true.
- **Regulated products** such as food, dietary supplements, cosmetics, medical devices and drugs fall under the **Food and Drug Administration**. Be extra careful with health and beauty claims.
- **The Ad Standards Council (ASC)** sets the industry's self-regulatory standards.
- **The Data Privacy Act (RA 10173)** applies if your campaign collects customers' personal details, for example through a contest form.

Disclosure should be **clear, prominent and close to the endorsement**: in the caption or said in the video, using plain words such as "Paid partnership with [Brand]", "Sponsored" or "Ad". The same summary flags vague tags like #sp or #collab, and disclosures hidden in the comments, as risky. If creators earn a commission, they should say so near the link.

This isn't legal advice. For regulated products, check the specific rules with a lawyer or the regulator before the campaign goes live.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many followers should a creator have for a small business campaign?**
There's no magic number. Many small businesses get the best value from nano and micro creators (about 1,000 to 100,000 followers) with an audience in the right city and niche, and strong comments.

**Should I pay in cash or in products?**
Products or experiences can work for smaller creators, especially for restaurants, salons and hotels. For anything that takes real time to make, or for bigger creators, expect to pay a fee. Agree on it in writing either way.

**How do I know if the campaign worked?**
Give each creator a unique code or link, and compare orders, bookings and inquiries against what you spent. Keep the creators who delivered and drop the ones who didn't.

**Do creators have to label paid posts?**
Yes, they should make it clear the post is paid, upfront, with words like "Paid partnership", "Sponsored" or "Ad". Ask for it in your brief so it's never an afterthought.
